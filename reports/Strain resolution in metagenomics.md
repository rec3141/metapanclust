# Match strain tools to divergence, depth, samples

Which strain-resolution method works depends on how far apart the genomes are, and the cut-off points are well defined. **Short-read assemblers lose most genomes that have a relative at ≥95% ANI.** **Standard long-read assemblers (metaFlye, metaMDBG) merge strains at about 98.6% ANI or higher.** **Long-read phasers (Strainberry, Strainy) separate strains down to about 99.5% ANI if each strain has at least 20× coverage.** Above about 99.5–99.9% ANI, only SNV haplotyping across many samples (DESMAN, STRONG) or matching against a reference database tells strains apart. For a danaSeq project with nanopore + Illumina co-assemblies and only a few samples per site, this means three things. Long-read phasing of individual bins (Strainy on a re-run metaFlye graph, or a myloasm re-assembly on R10 data) is the main tool for close species and well-diverged strains. Coverage deconvolution can assign accessory contigs to strains, but only when strain ratios really vary across samples, so pool across sites. SNV-only strains in novel environmental taxa will mostly give core-gene haplotypes, not whole genomes. CAMI II measured a 76-point drop in genome fraction for "common strain" genomes, and no binner benchmark sweeps ANI finer than 95%, so you will need to build an in-house ANI-ladder test. No published binner uses LiDAR or point-cloud methods. The closest is binny (density segmentation of a t-SNE map). The idea most worth testing, fitting "strain rays" in multi-sample coverage space with RANSAC, turns out to be a robust estimator for the same linear mixing model that DESMAN already uses.

## Every assembler collapses relatives; the question is at what ANI

The two CAMI rounds give the baseline. In CAMI I, short-read assemblers recovered genomes without a close relative almost completely: **MEGAHIT and Minia reached a median genome fraction of 98.2%**. For "common strains" (≥95% ANI to another genome) the best assembler, MEGAHIT, reached **only 22.5%**, and Meraga, Minia and VELOUR managed 12.0%, 11.6% and 4.1%. Binning completeness also collapsed for every program; MaxBin 2.0 was the only one to keep >90% purity. The authors warned that separating strains might need "substantially larger numbers of replicate samples than those analyzed here (up to 5)" ([Sczyrba et al. 2017, *Nat Methods*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5903868/)). CAMI II found the same pattern more sharply. On the strain-madness dataset, unique and common genomes differed by **79.1 points in strain recall, 75.9 points in genome fraction and 50-fold in NGA50**. Whole-dataset genome fraction was 10.4% for MEGAHIT, against 30.9% for the hybrid assembler A-STAR and 90.8% for the gold standard. The paper concludes that most assemblers "sometimes intentionally, did not resolve strain variation" and that "long reads help to distinguish strains" ([Meyer et al. 2022, *Nat Methods*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9007738/)). For binning on gold-standard assemblies, CONCOCT ranked best on common strains, Vamb had the highest overall purity, and unique strains binned "substantially better" than common ones in both datasets (same source). A 2025 evaluation ranked SemiBin2 and COMEBin best overall. On a CAMI2 strain-associated set, the embeddings separated genomes only 36–52% of the time (Vamb 35.9%, COMEBin 41.6%, McDevol 52.2%) ([bioRxiv 2025.02.21.639465](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.02.21.639465.full.pdf); figures from a search snippet, journal venue not verified).

Long reads raise the threshold but do not remove it. In the Strainberry paper, metaFlye merged *E. coli* K-12 and W, which share **98.65% identity**, into consensus sequences with duplication ratios of 1.23 and 1.39 ([Vicedomini et al. 2021, *Nat Commun*](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/88125/1/Vicedomini_etal_2021_NatureComms.pdf)). On the ZymoBIOMICS D6331 gut mock, five *E. coli* strains at 8% each, metaFlye produced no circular *E. coli* genome. hifiasm-meta produced one circular strain and one in two contigs, and HiCanu circularised two ([Feng et al. 2022, *Nat Methods*, arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.08457)). metaMDBG is built to remove strain variation: a "local progressive abundance filter" strips strain variability, and strain duplicates are purged after polishing. On the same mock it circularised one *E. coli* strain, and no assembler resolved all five ([search summary of the metaMDBG paper, PMC11392814](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11392814)). An independent 2026 comparison of 21 HiFi metagenomes reported up to 180× more read-clipping events (a proxy for assembly errors) for metaFlye and metaMDBG than for HiCanu and hifiasm-meta. It also flagged metaMDBG's high count of circular contigs as possible premature circularisation ([Trigodet et al. 2026, *Nat Biotechnol*](https://epic.awi.de/60560/1/Trigodet_2026_nature_biotechnology.pdf); read only through a search snippet and not verified).

Put together, the benchmarks give a sequence of thresholds. That sequence is the main framework for this report:

| Approach | Where separation fails | Depth needed | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-read de Bruijn assembly + binning | Broadly at ≥95% ANI to a relative | — | [CAMI I](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5903868/), [CAMI II](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9007738/) |
| metaFlye / metaMDBG (default) | Merges pairs at ~98.6% ANI; metaMDBG filters strain variation by design | — | [Strainberry](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/88125/1/Vicedomini_etal_2021_NatureComms.pdf), [metaMDBG summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11392814) |
| Long-read phasing (Strainberry) | Fails below ~0.4% divergence (~99.6% ANI); near-complete at 0.5% | ≥20× per strain, plateau at 30× | [Strainberry](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/88125/1/Vicedomini_etal_2021_NatureComms.pdf) |
| Short-read graph haplotyping (STRONG) | Tested at 0.05–5% SCG divergence; strains missed when too close in sequence or coverage profile | Missed strains below ~5.7× | [Quince et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8311964) |
| Reference-based strain ID | Cannot separate strains within a ~99.89% ANI cluster unless the search is hierarchical | 0.1–1× | [StrainScan](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10433603/) |

The table has a gap that matters here. **No published benchmark measures binners or short-read assemblers on a controlled ANI ladder** (97, 99, 99.5, 99.9%). CAMI groups everything at ≥95% together, and the 2025 binning evaluation does not split results by ANI either. Where along 95–99% a particular danaSeq binner starts to merge genomes is therefore unknown, and you can only find out by testing it.

## Long-read phasing reaches 99.5% ANI at 20–30× per strain

Post-assembly phasers take an existing assembly plus reads, which makes them the natural "bin first, then split" step. **Strainberry** is the best characterised. It runs repeated diploid separations (Longshot variant calling, HapCUT2 phasing, wtdbg2 re-assembly) on a strain-oblivious metaFlye or Canu assembly. In its divergence series, reference coverage improved markedly at **0.39% divergence and was near-complete at 0.50% (~99.5% ANI)**. Quality was "mediocre at 10×", needed **at least 20× for >95% reference representation and >99.8% identity**, and levelled off after 30×. Absolute depth mattered; uneven depth between strains did not. With 2–5 *E. coli* strains at 0.7–1.4% pairwise divergence, average reference coverage dropped from 95% at three strains to 75% at five. On mocks, Strainberry separated *E. coli* K-12/W to 99.97% identity, and two *S. aureus* strains at 97.61% identity to 99.97% ANI. On real data it recovered 20–118% more strain-specific sequence than conventional assemblies. It ran under 10 GB of RAM: on a human stool dataset, 4 h against Flye's 6.5 h and 132 GB ([Vicedomini et al. 2021](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/88125/1/Vicedomini_etal_2021_NatureComms.pdf)). Two caveats apply to danaSeq. The authors designed Strainberry for low-complexity metagenomes. And on their ONT (R9-era) whey-starter data, strain identity was **98.78–99.76% against 99.69–99.97% with PacBio**, because nanopore indels interfered with marker annotation (same source).

**Strainy** is the tool most directly compatible with a Flye pipeline. It takes a metaFlye assembly graph and reads, and its README recommends a specific assembly call: `flye --meta --keep-haplotypes --no-alt-contigs -i 0`. That means the graph is unpolished and haplotype bubbles are kept. Strainy supports HiFi and nanopore R9 (Guppy5+) and R10. By default it phases only unitigs with **20–500× coverage**, and it outputs strain contigs, a phased BAM and a strain VCF ([Strainy GitHub](https://github.com/katerinakazantseva/stRainy)). The paper says it outperforms "current Nanopore-based methods" and is comparable to PacBio-based tools ([Kazantseva et al. 2024, *Nat Methods*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39327484/)). The abstract gives no numbers, and this review could not extract its benchmark figures, so Strainy's divergence and depth limits have not been verified independently. Its licence (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) excludes commercial use. **Floria** phases reads that have been aligned to contigs and comes with a VCF. It accepts short or long reads, ran more than 3× faster than Strainberry with 21% more strain content recovered on synthetic data, and averaged under 20 min per sample across 109 deep nanopore metagenomes ([Shaw et al. 2024, *Bioinformatics* ISMB issue](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11211831/)).

Strain-aware assemblers work at assembly time instead. **myloasm** builds a string graph and simplifies it using polymorphic k-mers ("SNPmers") and differential abundance. The authors say it "can resolve up to 99% ANI quite confidently", where other nanopore assemblers "collapse similar genomes at > 95% ANI". They report six complete single-contig *Prevotella copri* genomes from one gut sample and about 3× more complete circular contigs than the next-best assembler on real ONT data. It needs **R10 simplex reads at ≥~97% accuracy or HiFi**, uses about the RAM of metaFlye (more than metaMDBG), and "may occasionally produce chimeric misassembled contigs" ([Shaw, Marin & Li 2025, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.05.674543.full.pdf); [myloasm docs](https://myloasm-docs.github.io/)). All of these numbers come from the authors. The GitHub README says the paper was published in *Nature Biotechnology* in 2026, which this review could not confirm. hifiasm-meta is HiFi-only, so it does not apply to nanopore data. The best-documented working floor for long-read phasing is therefore **about 99.5% ANI at 20–30× per strain**. None of the verified sources shows long reads alone separating strains above 99.9% ANI.

## Short-read SNV haplotyping trades samples for resolution

De novo SNV methods treat each sample as a different mixture of the same G strains. They recover haplotypes as the latent factors that explain how allele frequencies covary across samples, so **they only work when strain proportions vary between samples**. **DESMAN** starts from a binned co-assembly. It finds variant positions in single-copy core genes (SCGs), fits a non-negative tensor factorisation refined by Gibbs sampling, and then assigns non-core genes to strains. On a 64-sample mock with five *E. coli* strains it recovered all five, at **0.17–0.64% SNV error and R² = 0.9998 for frequencies**. On a 96-sample, 210-genome mock, gene presence/absence was 94.9% accurate (97.39% for haplotypes below 1% SNV error). It loses accuracy quickly with fewer samples: "The SNV error rate starts to increase when the sample number is below about 30; however, the average error is still around 15%, even with just ten samples", and with few samples "some of the strains are resolved accurately and others are missed completely." Strains were resolvable at about 100 SNPs (~0.38% SCG divergence), and sometimes with as few as ten ([Quince et al. 2017, *Genome Biol*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5607848)). Its repository was last updated in 2020.

**STRONG** decomposes per-sample unitig coverage on the unsimplified assembly graph of each MAG's SCGs, using a variational Bayesian method (BayesPaths). It makes much better use of a small number of samples. With few samples it still predicted strains at **0.068% per-base error, and below 0.05% with ten or more**. Across the synthetic sets it found 216 strains at 0.052% error, against DESMAN's 208 at 0.176% and mixtureS at 48.7%. The costs are high. STRONG needs its own metaSPAdes short-read co-assembly, so it **cannot take a Flye or metaMDBG MAG**. It resolves SCGs only and does not assign accessory genes. It tops out at about 8–9 strains per species. Its anaerobic-digester run took over 16 days on 64 threads. In a stability experiment, when the coefficient of variation of strain proportions fell from 1.45 to 0.23, haplotype error rose from "practically zero" to 0.38% ([Quince et al. 2021, *Genome Biol*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8311964)). STRONG's own nanopore cross-check is a useful model for danaSeq: two strains seen in nanopore reads matched STRONG's haplotypes at 100% identity over 2,313 bp, at a predicted frequency of 28.0% against 27.6% observed (same source).

The other short-read tools address narrower problems. **StrainFacts** factorises allele counts with "fuzzy" genotypes and scales to more than 10,000 samples. It depends on GT-Pro SNP sites from a reference database, performed poorly on a strain at 3% abundance, and degraded "dramatically" when the strain number was underestimated ([Smith et al. 2022, *Front Bioinform*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioinformatics/articles/10.3389/fbinf.2022.867386/full)). **inStrain** measures microdiversity and decides whether two samples carry the same strain (popANI ≥99.999% over ≥50% of the genome, ≥5× per compared base). It works on your own dereplicated MAGs but does not phase haplotypes ([Olm et al. 2021, *Nat Biotechnol*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9223867); [inStrain docs](https://instrain.readthedocs.io/en/latest/important_concepts.html)). **MetaStrainer** phases strains within a single sample against a MAG-like reference. It reached 92.1% correct variants, against mixtureS's 39.3%, and held up to about 8× coverage. It caps out at three strains, falls to 64.2% when strain frequencies are equal, and merges strains at ≥99.5% ANI ([Sharaf & Bobay 2026, *Bioinformatics*](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/42/6/btag340/8691842)). Reference profilers (StrainGE at 0.1×, StrainScan at ~1× with about 20% better F1 on multi-strain samples) need close references ([van Dijk et al. 2022, *Genome Biol*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35255937/); [Liao et al. 2023, *Microbiome*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10433603/)). Novel environmental lineages will mostly lack such references. Even on *E. coli*, six profilers gave noticeably different compositions in a 2026 benchmark ([bioRxiv 2026.05.19.726160](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.05.19.726160v2); authors not verified).

## Coverage deconvolution is one linear model with a geometric catch

Every multi-sample deconvolution tool fits the same model: feature coverage ≈ Σ over strains of (presence indicator × strain abundance). DESMAN's accessory-gene step states it exactly. Gene coverage is Poisson with mean λ_{f,s} = Σ_g η_{f,g} γ_{g,s}, where η is binary presence and γ_{g,s} is strain coverage in sample s. The authors note that the method "could equally well be applied to either contigs or genes" ([Quince et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5607848)). The tools differ in what they use as features. STRONG uses graph unitigs. StrainPanDA uses gene-family depth against a reference pangenome, fitted by sparse NMF, and recommends **at least 10 samples and species relative abundance above 1%**. It struggles with strains "lacking strain-unique features" ([Hu et al. 2022, *iMeta*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10989911)). MSPminer clusters co-abundant genes using a proportionality measure that separates species core from accessory genes, with input filtered to genes having >6 reads in ≥3 samples ([Plaza Oñate et al. 2019, *Bioinformatics*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6499236)). MIDAS v3 + StrainPGC call gene content per strain from the ratio of gene depth to species depth plus a correlation score across samples. They note that strain-specific genes show a "decreased depth ratio … when other strains of the same species are also abundant" ([Smith et al. 2025, *Genome Res*](https://genome.cshlp.org/content/35/5/1247)). The two kinds of signal complement each other. Gene-content methods cannot split strains with identical accessory genomes, and SNV methods cannot split strains that are identical on the core.

The geometry explains why binners merge strains and when deconvolution works. Each contig's coverage vector across S samples lies in the cone spanned by the k strain abundance vectors. Core contigs lie on the sum ray, strain-specific contigs on the extreme rays, and contigs shared by a subset of strains on faces of the cone. Correlation-based binners cluster by angle, so when strains co-vary they put the whole cone into one bin. Finding the strains then means finding the extreme rays, which is separable NMF or archetypal analysis ([arXiv 1405.4275](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.4275)). The solution is unique only under "separability" (anchor samples or anchor contigs for each strain) or the weaker "sufficiently scattered" condition ([Fu, Huang & Sidiropoulos 2018, *IEEE Signal Process Lett*, arXiv 1709.00614](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.00614)). Linear algebra alone requires **at least as many samples as strains, with linearly independent abundance profiles**. If strain ratios are constant, all strains fall on one ray and no amount of depth separates them. This follows from the model and matches STRONG's stability experiment. For danaSeq this geometry argues for co-analysing samples from different sites. A site where one strain is absent is an anchor sample, which is the best case for identifiability, whereas technical replicates or closely spaced time points add almost nothing. As a rule of thumb from the benchmarks, strains below about 5× per sample or about 5% relative frequency are often missed ([STRONG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8311964); [StrainPanDA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10989911)). No source gives a closed-form power analysis linking sample count, depth and the angle between strain profiles.

The ratio of accessory-contig coverage to core coverage estimates the fraction of a species' coverage contributed by strains that carry the contig. Plasmid and IS copy number confound it: a value between 0 and 1 can mean "carried by a subset at one copy", and a value above 1 means a multicopy element. Coverage from a single sample cannot tell these apart. The test that works is whether a contig's ratio tracks one strain's abundance profile across samples, which is the logic behind both StrainPGC and DESMAN ([StrainPGC](https://genome.cshlp.org/content/35/5/1247)).

## Point-cloud methods are unexplored, and the useful parts already have names

This review found **no paper or preprint applying LiDAR or 3D point-cloud pipelines (PCL filtering, RANSAC, Euclidean cluster extraction, PointNet) to metagenomic binning**. The nearest existing work treats contigs as points in a density space. binny embeds CLR-transformed k-mer and coverage features with openTSNE using a multi-scale perplexity kernel. It then segments the map with HDBSCAN (which has a noise class), pulls out clusters that pass CheckM, re-embeds the remaining contigs, and lowers the minimum contig length step by step from coarse to fine ([Hickl et al. 2022, *Brief Bioinform*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9677464)). In point-cloud terms that is iterative segmentation with outlier handling and a voxel-size-like schedule. VizBin offered a manual 2D t-SNE scatter ([Laczny et al. 2015, *Microbiome*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4305225)). GroopM plotted contigs in a "transformed coverage profile space" with circle size scaled by contig length. It could not resolve strains and merged two *S. epidermidis* strains into one bin ([Imelfort et al. 2014, *PeerJ*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183954/)). Whether GroopM's coring used a Hough-like transform is not verified. Topological data analysis has been applied to multi-mapping reads ([Guzmán-Sáenz et al. 2019, *BMC Genomics*](https://bmcgenomics.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12864-019-5490-y)) and to sample-level microbiome profiles, but not to contig binning. PointNet-style networks have been applied to gene expression and smFISH spots, also not to contigs ([PointFISH, arXiv 2302.10923](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.10923)).

Two transfers have no precedent in binning and fit the strain problem. The first is **RANSAC ray and cone fitting in multi-sample coverage space**. Fit and remove the dominant species ray, the analogue of "ground removal". Then fit two-ray cones to what remains: contigs that are inliers to a cone but to neither ray are candidate shared core, and inliers to a single ray are strain-specific. This is a robust estimator for the model in the previous section (DESMAN's λ = Σ η γ). It is not a new model, but it adds what NMF lacks: explicit rejection of outliers such as mobile elements, chimeras and GC-biased contigs, and length-weighted voting. The second is **local eigenvalue shape features** (linearity, planarity, scattering) computed on kNN neighbourhoods, as in LiDAR classification ([Weinmann et al. 2015, *ISPRS J Photogramm Remote Sens*](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/semantic-point-cloud-interpretation-based-on-optimal-neighborhood/)). These could flag bins with planar coverage structure, meaning a mixture of two abundance vectors. Marker-gene checks cannot see such a mixture when the assembler has merged the shared core into one consensus copy, because every marker then appears once (an inference from how collapsed assemblies behave, not a published result). A third, cheaper idea is to embed contigs with many seeds, align the runs with Procrustes using contig IDs as correspondences, and score each contig by how often it lands in its bin. Low-agreement contigs are candidate MGEs or misbinned strain-variable regions. scStability already does this for single-cell embeddings ([CRAN scStability](https://stat.ethz.ch/CRAN/web/packages/scStability/scStability.pdf)). All of these should run in raw log-coverage space, not in t-SNE or UMAP space, because the embedding bends rays into filaments. None has been tested: there is no evidence that they beat NMF, and coverage biases at shared regions could break the linear model they rely on.

## Regime-specific recommendations for danaSeq

| Within-genus regime | Signature | What works | Samples / depth | danaSeq action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Well-separated species (<95% ANI) | Different TNF and coverage; CheckM-clean bins | Standard multi-sample binning (SemiBin2/COMEBin/CONCOCT) | Already met | Bin, dereplicate at 95% ANI; no strain step |
| Close species with distinct accessory genes (95–99% ANI) | Merged or chimeric bins; Flye merges at ~98.6% | Long-read phasing or myloasm re-assembly; gene/contig coverage deconvolution for accessory assignment | Phasing ≥20× per strain; deconvolution ≥10 samples with varying ratios, species ≥1% | Strainy on per-bin metaFlye `--keep-haplotypes -i 0`; myloasm if R10 sup; accessory-contig ratio model across sites |
| Strains differing mainly by SNVs (>99–99.5% ANI) | One consensus MAG; high inStrain nucleotide diversity | Floria / MetaStrainer per sample; DESMAN or STRONG across samples; inStrain popANI for tracking | Floria/Strainy ≥20×; DESMAN ≥~30 samples; STRONG ≥~3–10 but needs a metaSPAdes graph | Accept core-gene haplotypes; use inStrain to track strain sharing between sites |

Start each multi-species or multi-strain genus with a cheap triage. Compute pairwise ANI among the genus's MAGs (skani is in the danaSeq container), and run inStrain on the dereplicated MAGs across all samples to see which bins carry microdiversity and which strains are shared between sites. That places each genus in a regime before any expensive tool runs. For each candidate bin, estimate per-strain depth as bin coverage × expected minor-strain fraction, and skip phasing where that falls below about 20×. Strainberry's series and Strainy's defaults both put the floor there.

For close species and strains at ≤99.5% ANI, long-read phasing is the main lever, because a few samples per site cannot power DESMAN. Strainy needs an unpolished `--keep-haplotypes --no-alt-contigs` metaFlye graph. The production assembly does not meet that, so the realistic route is a bin-scoped Flye re-run on recruited nanopore reads. That route is an inference; this review found no published test of it. If the reads are R10 sup-basecalled, myloasm is a strong alternative re-assembly at the same per-bin scale, but its strain claims come only from its authors. Do not expect strain content from metaMDBG bins, which filter strain variation by design. Use them for species recovery and do the strain step on Flye/myloasm graphs.

To assign accessory contigs, implement the DESMAN-style model directly on co-assembly contigs. Normalise each contig's coverage by the median of the bin's SCG contigs in each sample. Estimate strain abundance profiles γ from phased long-read haplotypes or SNVs where they exist. Then fit binary presence per contig with γ fixed. Pool samples across sites so the abundance vectors are scattered, and treat contigs whose ratio exceeds 1 as candidate multicopy MGEs rather than strain markers. This is also a natural place to try the RANSAC ray and cone model as a robust alternative, with DESMAN-style NMF as the comparison.

Because no published benchmark sweeps ANI below CAMI's 95% split, build one. Spike simulated reads from real danaSeq MAGs and their mutated or recombined copies at 97, 99, 99.5 and 99.9% ANI and at 10, 20 and 40× per strain into a real sample. Then measure where each binner and phaser fails. The D6331 five-*E. coli* mock is the shared external test. Validate short-read haplotypes against nanopore reads on high-coverage SCGs, as STRONG did. Accept that SNV-only strains of novel taxa will come out as core-gene haplotypes plus inStrain sharing calls, not closed genomes.

## Conclusion

The strain problem in this project breaks into three sub-problems that need different data. Separation by sequence divergence needs long reads at depth. Separation by gene content needs samples whose strain ratios differ. Separation by SNVs alone needs many such samples, or reference genomes the project will not have. The most consequential design choice is therefore sampling, not algorithm: samples drawn across sites, where strains appear and disappear, give the "anchor" geometry that makes coverage deconvolution identifiable, whereas more depth on similar samples does not. The second is that strain resolution has to be a separate per-bin stage with its own assembly graph, because the production assemblers (and metaMDBG most of all) are built to produce species consensus.

The point-cloud angle fits better than it first appears. LiDAR's practical strength is robust fitting of simple geometric models amid outliers, and the strain-mixture model is a simple geometric model (rays and cones in coverage space) whose main failure modes are outliers: mobile elements, chimeras and biased contigs. A RANSAC ray/cone fitter benchmarked against DESMAN-style NMF on the in-house ANI ladder is a small, publishable experiment that fills a gap no binner has addressed. Until it is tested, it stays a hypothesis.
